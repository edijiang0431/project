## Audience 1: IFAD Program Officer 

**Decision:**
A program officer at the International Fund for Agricultural Development (IFAD) must decide which three remittance corridors to put a cost-reduction program into next year. 

### Current decision process hypothesis 
The following process is a hypothesis based on AI role play and should not be interpreted as IFAD's confirmed actual procedure. 

### Possible Trigger 
A decision like this might be triggered by a planning or funding cycle, by evidence that certain remittance corridors continue to have high costs, or by a need to identify new areas for remittance-related intervention.

### Information that might be used
A program officer might consider: 
- Remittance cost data by corridor and over time
- Fee and foreign-exchange margin information
- Remittance flow or market-size information
- Financial inclusion and digital-payment conditions
- Local regulations
- Partner and implementation capacity
- Input from local stakeholders

### People who might be involved
The decision may involve: 
- IFAD program or remittance specialists
- Regional or country teams
- Government or regulatory counterparts
- Financial institutions or payment providers
- Local implementing partners
- Monitoring and evaluation staff

### Possible current process
1. Identify remittance corridors with relatively high or persistent costs.
2. Review whether the cost pattern remains consistent over time.
3. Combine price information with external factors such as remittance importance, local conditions, and implementation feasibility.
4. Consult relevant stakeholders or partners.
5. Assess potential interventions, risks, and available resources.
6. Compare the shortlisted corridors and select priorities.

### Difficulties or uncertainties
- After identifying high-cost corridors, the officer may still need to determine whether those corridors are important enough to justify a program intervention.
- A high-cost corridor does not automatically show what problem the program should address.
- The officer may need to assess whether a realistic cost-reduction intervention can actually be implemented in each shortlisted corridor.
- Information from local or delivery partners could change which corridors appear most practical to prioritize.

### Evidence check

- IFAD and its Financing Facility for Remittances (FFR) use remittance-cost research, including World Bank RPW data, and work on corridor-focused remittance projects. However, we did not verify that RPW rankings are formally used to select program corridors.
  [IFAD — Financing Facility for Remittances](https://www.ifad.org/en/remittances/financing-facility-for-remittances)

- Some FFR project-selection processes use multiple criteria such as impact, sustainability, implementation strategy, implementation capacity, and partnerships. This supports the idea that IFAD may consider feasibility and partner capacity in addition to cost.
  [IFAD/FFR — South Africa Guidelines](https://www.ifad.org/documents/d/new-ifad.org/call-remittances-southafrica-guidelines-pdf)

- IFAD sources show that remittance project design and selection can involve IFAD/FFR staff and external partners. However, the exact decision owner and approval process for prioritizing corridors remains unverified.

### Open questions 
- Does IFAD use RPW data when prioritizing remittance corridors?
- What criteria does IFAD use to prioritize one corridor over another?
- Who is involved in and approves this prioritization decision?



## Audience 2: CHIRLA Campaign Researcher

**Decision:**  
A campaign researcher at CHIRLA must decide which United States remittance corridor to use for a “know what you really pay” campaign.

### Current decision process hypothesis

The following process is a hypothesis based on AI role play and should not be interpreted as CHIRLA's confirmed actual procedure.

### Possible trigger

A decision like this might be triggered by campaign planning, concerns from community members about confusing remittance costs, or evidence that certain U.S. remittance corridors show large differences in quoted costs across sending methods.

### Information that might be used

A campaign researcher might consider:

- Remittance costs by corridor
- Visible fees and foreign exchange margins
- Differences between counter and internet access points
- Provider and payment method information
- Input from community members and partner organizations
- Existing consumer education materials on remittance pricing

### People who might be involved

The decision may involve:

- Campaign research staff
- Communications staff
- Community outreach or engagement staff
- CHIRLA leadership
- Community partners
- People who send remittances

### Possible current process

1. Identify U.S. origin remittance corridors with notable cost differences.
2. Compare total cost, fees, FX margins, and access points.
3. Shortlist corridors where the pricing issue is clear enough to communicate.
4. Consult community-facing staff or partners to determine whether those corridors are relevant to CHIRLA's audience.
5. Develop and check a consumer education message.
6. Select a corridor and campaign focus.

### Difficulties or uncertainties

- A corridor with a large difference in quoted total cost between counter and internet access points may not be the most relevant corridor for CHIRLA's community.
- The campaign researcher may need community input to understand which sending methods and destinations matter most.
- A pricing difference that is statistically noticeable may still be difficult to explain clearly in a public campaign.
- The researcher may need to balance data findings with audience relevance and campaign usefulness.

### Evidence check

- CHIRLA provides community education related to financial literacy and consumer protection through workshops, presentations, and outreach. This supports the idea that a remittance price education campaign could fit its broader work. 

    However, we did not find evidence that CHIRLA currently runs a campaign specifically focused on remittance fees, FX margins, or exchange rate transparency. Therefore, the “know what you really pay” campaign remains a hypothetical use case.

  [CHIRLA — Community Education & Outreach](https://www.chirla.org/what-we-do/programs-initiatives/community-education-outreach/)

  [CHIRLA — Consumer Protections for Immigrants](https://www.chirla.org/what-we-do/our-priority-issues/consumer-protections-for-immigrants/)

- CHIRLA's outreach and organizing work relies on community networks, members, and partner organizations. This supports the hypothesis that community relevance and partner input could influence campaign planning. 

    However, we did not verify a specific process for selecting remittance-related campaign topics.

  [CHIRLA — Organizing & Leadership Development](https://www.chirla.org/what-we-do/programs-initiatives/organizing-leadership-development/)
