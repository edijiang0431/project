# Role 1 — Objectives

## Audience 1: IFAD Program Officer

**Decision:** Decide which three remittance corridors should receive a cost-reduction program next year.

### What matters when making the decision?
- Identify corridors with relatively high remittance costs.
- Compare total cost across corridors and over time.
- Prioritize corridors where a cost-reduction program could have a meaningful impact.
- Consider differences across providers and access points.
- Use consistent data and measures when comparing corridors.

### What could go badly if the wrong decision is made?
- Program resources could be spent on corridors where costs are already relatively low.
- The program may have little effect on reducing remittance costs.
- A decision based on limited time periods could miss important trends.
- Resources could be directed away from corridors with greater need.

### What evidence would change their mind?
- A corridor's total cost is not actually high compared with other corridors.
- Recent data shows that costs are already decreasing without intervention.
- Another corridor has a larger and more consistent cost problem.
- Differences across providers or access points suggest that another type of intervention would be more effective.

### How would they measure success?
- A decrease in average total remittance cost.
- A decrease in fees and/or exchange-rate margins.
- Lower costs in the selected corridors over time.
- Greater cost reductions in targeted corridors compared with similar non-targeted corridors.

## Audience 2: CHIRLA Campaign Researcher

**Decision:** Decide which remittance corridor should be targeted for a "know what you really pay" campaign.

### What matters when making the decision?
- Identify a corridor where consumers face meaningful differences in remittance costs.
- Compare total costs across different access points.
- Identify differences that can be clearly explained to consumers.
- Use reliable evidence that can support a public-facing campaign.
- Consider whether the issue is relevant to the communities CHIRLA serves.

### What could go badly if the wrong decision is made?
- The campaign could focus on a corridor with only a small cost difference.
- CHIRLA could make a claim that the data does not strongly support.
- Consumers could misunderstand the difference between fees and exchange-rate costs.
- The campaign could target a corridor where the issue is less relevant than in another corridor.

### What evidence would change their mind?
- The cost difference between access points is smaller than expected.
- Another corridor shows a larger and more consistent difference.
- The difference disappears when looking across different time periods or providers.
- The available data is not strong enough to support a clear consumer message.

### How would they measure success?
- A clear and consistent difference in total cost across access points.
- Strong evidence that supports the campaign's main message.
- Increased consumer understanding of the actual cost of sending money.
- Campaign reach and engagement.
- Evidence that consumers are better able to compare remittance options.

## Comparison of the Two Audiences

| | IFAD Program Officer | CHIRLA Campaign Researcher |
|---|---|---|
| **Main goal** | Reduce remittance costs | Increase consumer awareness |
| **Decision** | Select three corridors for a cost-reduction program | Select one corridor for a consumer awareness campaign |
| **Main focus** | Where resources can have the greatest impact | Where consumers face meaningful cost differences |
| **Important evidence** | Total cost, trends, corridors, providers, access points | Total cost, access points, providers, corridors |
| **Measure of success** | Lower remittance costs | Better consumer understanding and strong campaign evidence |
| **Main risk** | Ineffective use of program resources | Weak or misleading campaign message |
